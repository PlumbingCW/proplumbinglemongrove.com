How House Plumbing Works: What Lemon Grove, CA Homes Need

Your plumbing is two systems that never mix: one brings clean water in, and the other carries wastewater out. Here is how they work together.

A house has two plumbing systems. The supply system brings pressurized water in from the street. The drain-waste-vent system carries wastewater out by gravity and keeps sewer gas from entering the home. They meet only at fixtures, where a faucet or a showerhead releases supply water and a drain takes the used water away.

Understanding that split explains why some problems affect only hot water, why some affect only drains and why a leak on one side never looks like a leak on the other.

The supply side

1

The meter

Water passes through a meter at the street. It belongs to the water utility.

2

The main valve

After the meter, a shutoff valve lets you stop water to the house.

3

The pressure regulator

Many homes have a valve that lowers street pressure to a safe level.

4

Branch lines

Pipes split to the water heater and to each fixture, usually with a cold and a hot line.

The water heater adds the hot side. Cold water enters the tank and leaves heated. The pipe pattern is cold on one side and hot on the other at every fixture.

The drain, waste and vent side

Every drain has a trap, a curved section that holds water and blocks sewer gas. Drains slope downward to larger pipes and join the main sewer line, which leaves the house toward the street.

Vents are pipes that rise through the roof. They let air into the drain system so water flows freely and traps do not siphon dry. A blocked vent can make drains gurgle or drain slowly.

Where problems show up

Supply leaks

Stains, low pressure, high bills and wet spots. Water runs even when nothing is open.

Drain clogs

Slow drains, gurgling and backups. The problem is always below the fixture.

Vent problems

Gurgling, slow drains in several fixtures and sewer smells.

Heater trouble

No hot water, rust-colored hot water or leaks at the base.

Things every homeowner should know

  • Where the main water shutoff is.
  • Where the cleanout is.
  • Where the water heater shutoffs are.
  • How old the pipes are.
  • Who maintains the sewer line past your property.

If you can name those five things, you are ahead of most homeowners. If you cannot, a plumber can walk the house with you in an hour.

Gas lines and venting, the other pipes

Gas appliances such as a water heater, a furnace and a range have a third set of pipes. Gas lines carry fuel to the appliance, and vent pipes carry exhaust to the outside. They are not part of the water plumbing, but a plumber often installs them.

Gas work needs care and a permit in most places. If you ever smell gas, leave the building and call 911 or your gas utility from outside. Do not look for the leak yourself.

Shutoffs at every fixture

Each sink, toilet and appliance should have its own shutoff valve. Those small valves let you repair a faucet or a toilet without closing the whole house. If your fixtures do not have them, adding them is one of the best small upgrades a plumber can do.

Why old houses differ from new ones

Older homes may have galvanized steel supply pipes and cast iron or clay drains. Newer homes use copper, PEX and plastic. The materials age differently and fail differently, which is why a plumber asks the age of the house when you call. Knowing the age gives a clue to the problem before anyone opens a wall.

A walk through the house

Start at the street and follow the water. It passes the meter, enters the house, meets the main valve and the regulator, and splits into branches. Follow the cold line to the water heater. From there, hot water leaves on its own branch. At each fixture, supply meets drain.

Now follow the waste. Water goes down the drain, through the trap, into a branch and then into the main drain. The vent rises beside it to the roof. A good plumber can trace every pipe in the house the same way.

A short glossary

  • Angle stop: the small valve under a sink or behind a toilet.
  • P-trap: the curved pipe under a sink that holds a water seal.
  • Cleanout: a capped pipe used to clear a drain.
  • Regulator: a valve that lowers street pressure for the house.
  • Aerator: the small screen at the end of a faucet.

Codes and licensing to know about

Plumbing in California is governed by the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC). Whether a project like this needs a permit depends on the scope, so ask the City of Lemon Grove building department before you start. Contractors hold a C-36 plumbing license from the Contractors State License Board, which you can verify at cslb.ca.gov.

Plumbing in Lemon Grove: local notes

Helix Water District supplies Lemon Grove and its meter marks the end of the district's responsibility on the supply side. On the drain side, the line from your house to the public sewer is generally yours, and the City of Lemon Grove public works office can tell you who maintains the main. Ask before you assume.

If you are curious how old your plumbing is, the age of the house helps. Lemon Grove was subdivided in 1892 and has grown across the decades since. Homes of several eras sit side by side, and pipes follow suit.
