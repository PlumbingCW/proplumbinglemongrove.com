Three eras of La Mesa homes, three kinds of plumbing

La Mesa incorporated in 1912, when the community was still called La Mesa Springs and had about 700 residents. The railroad that arrived in 1889 brought the first subdivisions and lemon ranches, and a land boom between 1906 and 1912 platted about a thousand lots in the central part of town.

A cottage near the Village that dates from those years may still have galvanized supply pipe and cast iron drains. Both rust from the inside, so the usual signs are discolored water, falling pressure and repeat clogs. If that sounds familiar, a sewer camera inspection shows what the drain line looks like today.

The next wave came after World War II. La Mesa's population rose from 3,925 in 1940 to over 50,000 by 1980, as suburban streets spread north and west of the old downtown. Homes from that period commonly have copper supply lines with cast iron or clay drains, and many sit on concrete slabs, so slab leaks and root-bound sewer laterals are the calls we expect there.

Hillside streets near Grossmont and Mount Helix add a third factor. Elevation changes water pressure from one street to the next, and homes at the bottom of a slope carry more pressure than homes at the top. A failed pressure regulator on a low street can burst a supply line, which is a job for burst pipe repair.

Water supply and what to check

Helix Water District supplies La Mesa, and its office is on University Avenue in La Mesa. It publishes an annual water quality report. We do not state a hardness number on this page, because the report is the right source. If scale builds on fixtures or in your heater, ask Helix for the report or test your tap.

Plumbing work in La Mesa follows the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), and permits come from the local building department. Scale and sediment shorten the life of a tank water heater. If yours is past ten years, see water heater replacement before it fails at night.
